I would redesign Shift around a three-stage loop
UNDERSTAND
The home screen doesn't start with:
"What mental-health condition do you have?"
It starts with:
What's going on?
The user can type naturally:
"My friend hasn't texted me back since yesterday and I keep checking my phone. I know I'm probably being stupid but I feel weird."
Shift processes that into a clean breakdown.
What happened
Observation
Your friend has not responded since yesterday.
What you may be feeling
Shift might offer:
anxious
uncertain
rejected
embarrassed
frustrated
Then:
"Anxiety and uncertainty seem most consistent with what you described. Does that fit?"
The user confirms or changes it.
What your mind is adding
Observation: They haven't responded.
Interpretation: They're losing interest in me.
That distinction becomes visual.
What may matter underneath it
Connection and predictability appear important here.
Possible protective rule
Working hypothesis:
"If somebody becomes distant, I need to do something quickly or I may lose the relationship."
Crucially:
Does this fit?
✅ Yes
❌ No
✏️ Change it
The AI never gets to declare the person's history for them.
Perspective shift
Then Shift produces something believable rather than forced positivity:
Old perspective:
"Silence means something is wrong and I need to fix it."
Updated perspective:
"Silence gives me incomplete information. I can care about the relationship without treating uncertainty as proof that I'm losing it."
And finally:
Choice:
"Don't send another message tonight. See what actually happens."
That is Stage 1.
PRACTICE
Now comes the part that can make Shift genuinely different.
Instead of saying:
"Great. Remember that."
Shift says:
Let's train it.
And it generates an arcade based on the cognitive skill the person needs to practice.
Not an arcade based on the trauma itself.
Example: Fact vs. Story
The user gets a retro arcade screen.
Statements fall from the top.
They have to move them into:
FACT
or
STORY
Examples:
"They haven't replied in 20 hours."
FACT
"They're deliberately ignoring me."
STORY
"They read my message."
FACT, assuming the user actually knows that.
"They think I'm annoying."
STORY
As the player progresses, the examples become slightly more ambiguous.
That is basically training the distinction between observation and interpretation through repetition.
And it doesn't feel like doing a worksheet.
REINFORCE
This is where I think the product gets particularly interesting.
After the person leaves that session, Shift remembers:
Patrick — or any user — is currently practicing:
Uncertainty ≠ rejection
Three days later, the dashboard might say:
2-minute refresher available
They play another short round.
A week later:
"Do you still agree with this updated perspective?"
And eventually:
What did you predict?
"They weren't going to talk to me again."
What actually happened?
"They texted me the next morning."
Now the learning loop has evidence from the person's own life.
Your trauma-processing framework already uses this prediction-versus-outcome concept because repeated new evidence is more useful than simple reassurance.
That becomes a major Shift feature.

Each game can have several difficulty levels.
One of my favorites would be "Both Can Be True"
This would directly reinforce one of the strongest principles in the framework:
Understanding explains behavior. It does not decide my boundary.
The player gets two apparently conflicting statements.
Example:
"My friend may have been overwhelmed."
and:
"It still hurt that they didn't call."
They have to identify:
BOTH CAN BE TRUE
Another:
"My coworker may not have intended to embarrass me."
"I don't want them to make that joke again."
Again:
BOTH CAN BE TRUE
That trains something considerably more useful than generic positive thinking.
Another game: "Me First"
This one is particularly close to what we developed in your project, but it generalizes beautifully.
A character does something upsetting.
The game asks questions in order.
Round 1
Why did they do it?
The player tries to answer.
BUZZER.
Too early.
Next:
What happened?
Player answers.
What did you feel?
Player answers.
What did you want instead?
Player answers.
Was it acceptable to you?
Player answers.
Then:
What might explain their behavior?
Now they can analyze the other person.
The game is literally training the order:
Feel before explain.
Your project source expressly describes the goal as changing the sequence so that emotion, need, and boundary are noticed before analysis takes over.
"Perspective Flip" could be a puzzle game
Suppose someone's automatic thought is:
"My boss corrected me, therefore he thinks I'm incompetent."
Shift generates several tiles:
"He thinks I'm incompetent."
"He noticed one mistake."
"He may correct everyone."
"He may be stressed."
"I don't know why he corrected me."
"I made a mistake."
"One mistake means I'm bad at my job."
The player sorts them:
KNOWN
I made a mistake.
He corrected it.
POSSIBLE
He may be stressed.
He may correct everyone.
ASSUMED
He thinks I'm incompetent.
OVERGENERALIZED
One mistake means I'm bad at my job.
That's CBT-style cognitive differentiation without handing someone a worksheet and telling them they're doing CBT.
The game doesn't always have to be about thoughts
We can also train emotional vocabulary.
Emotion Decoder
Instead of:
"How are you feeling?"
Which is surprisingly difficult for a lot of people, Shift gives signals.
"You wanted to leave."
"Your face felt hot."
"You kept replaying what they said."
"You wished you had said something."
Possible emotions appear:
Embarrassment
Anger
Fear
Sadness
There isn't always one correct answer.
The game can say:
Anger and embarrassment could both fit. Which one feels closer?
That preserves the framework's rule that Shift should offer plausible emotion labels rather than telling somebody what they "really" feel.

We can gamify behavior without gamifying pain
This distinction should be a hard product rule.
I would never give someone:
🔥 7-day PTSD streak!
or:
🏆 You processed your childhood abuse! +500 XP
That would be inappropriate.
Instead:
Perspective Practice — Level 3
Distinguished observation from assumption 18 times.
Completed 5 prediction/outcome reviews.
Practiced 4 boundary decisions.
Reward skills and participation, not symptom severity or trauma disclosure.
